\subsection{Quantization Robustness of Small MoE Models}
\label{ch-quant}

\paragraph{Background}

\paragraph{Uniform affine quantization}

Post-training quantization typically maps each weight $x$ to a $b$-bit
integer via an affine transform with scale $s$ and zero-point $z$:
{\small
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
x_q &= \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\mathrm{round}\!\left(\frac{x}{s}\right) + z,
\; 0, \; 2^b - 1\right), \\
\hat{x} &= s\,(x_q - z),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}}
with $s = (\max x - \min x)/(2^b - 1)$ over a calibration range. For
weights roughly uniform within the range, the mean squared
reconstruction error is approximately $s^2/12$---quadratic in the
range. Two consequences follow that matter below: error grows with the
weight range, and per-group scales (small $s$ per group) beat
per-tensor scales whenever ranges vary across groups. GPTQ
\citep{frantar2023gptq} improves on round-to-nearest by correcting each
quantized weight against the inverse Hessian of the layer's inputs;
LLM.int8() \citep{dettmers2022llm} isolates outlier feature dimensions
in higher precision; SmoothQuant \citep{xiao2023smoothquant} migrates
quantization difficulty from activations to weights. All three were
developed on dense models at billion-plus scale.

\paragraph{Sparse mixture-of-experts}

In a sparse MoE layer, each token's hidden state $h$ is routed by a
small linear map to logits $r = W_r h$ over $E$ experts; a top-$k$
operation selects the experts that process the token, and their outputs
are combined by softmax weights \citep{shazeer2017moe}. Only $k \ll E$
experts activate per token, so capacity scales with $E$ while compute
scales with $k$. The router $W_r$ is tiny---in our reference model,
$d_{\text{model}} = 512$ and $E = 8$ give $512 \times 8 = 4{,}096$
parameters---but it controls the fate of millions of expert parameters.

\paragraph{Reference model}

Our running example is a 44M-parameter recurrent MoE transformer:
43,974,722 parameters total, hidden dimension 512, 6 prelude and 2 coda
layers, 4 recurrent loops, 8 experts per MoE layer, grouped-query
attention \citep{ainslie2023gqa}, GPT-2 BPE vocabulary of 50,257,
trained on TinyStories \citep{eldan2023tinystories} for 120M tokens
(loss 9.5242 $\rightarrow$ 1.5263). We also compute footprints for the
133M (133,334,082 parameters) and 500M (507,581,506 parameters)
configurations in the same family.

\paragraph{Why MoE quantizes differently}

\paragraph{Per-expert calibration skew}

In a dense layer, every weight serves every token, so calibration data
is the full distribution. In MoE, expert $e$ is calibrated only on the
tokens routed to it. Routing is deliberately skewed---load-balancing is
approximate, and some experts inevitably specialise narrowly---so
experts see different token counts, different activation statistics,
and different effective weight ranges. Since quantization error scales
with $s^2$ and $s$ with the range, rarely-fired experts calibrated on
thin data carry noisier scales and larger reconstruction error:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{E}_e = \frac{\|W_e - \hat{W}_e\|_F^2}{\|W_e\|_F^2}
\end{equation}
should vary systematically with expert utilisation, not randomly. The
prediction: per-expert relative error anti-correlates with expert
firing frequency. A dense model has no such structure; its error is
approximately uniform across the layer.

\paragraph{Router precision and the top-$k$ discontinuity}
\label{sec:router-disc}

The router is 4,096 parameters out of 44 million---under 0.01\%---and
it is the worst place in the model to quantize aggressively. The reason
is the top-$k$ operator: it is discontinuous. A perturbation
$\delta$ in router logits that merely rescales softmax probabilities
in a dense model can \emph{flip} expert selection here, rerouting the
token to an entirely different expert and changing the output by
$O(1)$, not $O(\delta)$. Formally, where the dense sensitivity is
Lipschitz in the weights, the MoE sensitivity has jump
discontinuities at every logit tie. The practical prescription is
mixed precision by construction: keep the router (and its input
normalisation) at 16 bits regardless of what happens elsewhere. The
cost is negligible---8\,KB---and the protection is structural.

\paragraph{The embedding table dominates}

This is the single most important quantitative fact about quantizing
small language models, MoE or otherwise. The embedding table has
$50{,}257 \times 512 = 25{,}731{,}584$ parameters: \textbf{58.5\% of
the entire 44M model}. More than half the weights are a lookup table.
Embedding quantization behaves differently from matmul quantization:
there is no accumulation of products, only selection, so the error
model is per-row rather than per-multiply---but the rows for rare
tokens are updated rarely during training and may carry noisier values,
mirroring the expert-skew argument. The standard practice of keeping
embeddings at higher precision is well motivated here twice over:
they are most of the model, and their rows are unevenly trained.

\paragraph{Shared dense versus expert parameters}

The prelude and coda layers are dense and shared across all tokens;
their quantization error is the familiar uniform kind. The MoE layers
hold most non-embedding parameters and carry the structured
sensitivities above. A sound quantization scheme therefore treats
three parameter classes differently---dense trunk, experts, router---not
one.

These are exact multiplications of the parameter counts; they exclude
scale-factor overhead, which for per-group INT4 (group size 128, FP16
scales) adds roughly $P/128 \times 2$ bytes---about 0.3\,MB at 44M,
negligible.

\paragraph{A concrete mixed-precision proposal.} Keep the embedding
table (25.7M params) and router at FP16; quantize everything else to
INT4:
{\small
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\underbrace{25.7\text{M} \times 2\,\text{B}}_{51.5\,\text{MB}}
\;+\;
\underbrace{18.2\text{M} \times 0.5\,\text{B}}_{9.1\,\text{MB}} \\
&\qquad\approx\; 60.6\,\text{MB},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}}
versus 88.0\,MB for uniform FP16---a 31\% saving that spends precision
exactly where the router-discontinuity argument
argued above says it matters. Whether the INT4
experts hold up is precisely what the protocol below is designed to
test.

A note on the KV cache: grouped-query attention already compresses it,
and cache quantization is orthogonal to weight quantization---we scope
this paper to weights.

\paragraph{Proposed evaluation protocol}

We propose the following measurements, none yet performed. For each
precision setting (FP16 baseline, INT8 uniform, INT4 uniform, and the
mixed-precision scheme above):

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Held-out perplexity} on a fixed TinyStories validation
split, reported per checkpoint-equivalent (post-training quantization
needs no retraining, so this is one number per setting).
\item \textbf{Per-expert relative error} $\mathcal{E}_e$ as defined
above, correlated against expert firing frequency measured on
the validation set. Hypothesis: strong negative correlation.
\item \textbf{Router flip rate:} the fraction of validation tokens
whose top-$k$ expert set changes between FP16 and quantized router,
\begin{equation}
F = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\mathbf{1}\{\mathrm{topk}(\tilde{r}_t) \neq \mathrm{topk}(r_t)\}.
\end{equation}
Hypothesis: $F$ stays near zero when the router is held at FP16 and
jumps discontinuously when it is quantized---the signature of the
router-discontinuity argument developed in Section~\ref{ch:arch}.
\item \textbf{Generation spot checks:} fixed prompts completed at each
precision, judged for coherence degradation. Catches failures that
perplexity averages hide.
\end{enumerate}

The protocol is designed so that a null result is informative: if
per-expert error shows no correlation with utilisation, the
calibration-skew argument is wrong, and uniform quantization is safer
for MoE than we claim.

\paragraph{Discussion}

The small-MoE case is the interesting one. In a trillion-parameter MoE,
experts are redundant---losing one to quantization noise is absorbed by
the others. At 44M with 8 experts, every expert is load-bearing; there
is no redundancy to hide behind. This inverts the usual intuition that
small models are easier to quantize: the \emph{weights} are fewer, but
the \emph{structure} is more fragile. The embedding dominance compounds
this: most of what you are quantizing is a lookup table, not compute.

The broader lesson is that quantization schemes should be
architecture-aware. Per-tensor INT8 treats a router logit and an expert
weight as the same kind of number; they are not.

\paragraph{Limitations}

This paper is analytical: no quantization experiment has been run, and
every sensitivity claim is a prediction, not a measurement. The memory
numbers are exact arithmetic, but real deployments add runtime,
activation, and cache overheads we do not model. Our expert-skew
argument assumes imperfect load balancing, which holds in practice but
is a property of training, not of the architecture. Finally, we scope
to weight quantization; activation and KV-cache quantization at small
scale deserve their own analysis.

\paragraph{Conclusion}

Small sparse-MoE models have a quantization profile distinct from dense
models: skewed per-expert calibration, a discontinuous router that
demands high precision despite its size, and an embedding table that is
most of the model. We derived these sensitivities, computed exact
memory footprints from 22\,MB to 2\,GB across three scales, proposed a
mixed-precision scheme at 60.6\,MB for the 44M model, and specified a
protocol---including a router flip-rate metric---that can confirm or
refute the analysis. The measurements are the obvious next step.
